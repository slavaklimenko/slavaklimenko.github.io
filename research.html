Research

Gas and dust as tracers of galaxy evolution

I use spectroscopy of bright background sources — distant quasars and stars in nearby galaxies — to study diffuse material in foreground galaxies through absorption and dust extinction.

At large distances, direct observations of the faint emission from diffuse gas are often difficult to obtain. In contrast, absorption spectroscopy provides precise measurements of material along a single line of sight, including column densities, velocity structure, species, extinction, and physical conditions. These precise measurements of local properties are often inaccessible to emission studies, which are able to measure only the integrated emission from multiple regions within the beam.

01

Molecular gas in the early Universe

I study molecular gas — H2, HD, and CO — in the interstellar medium of distant galaxies at cosmic noon (z ∼ 2–3). At these redshifts, the molecules’ ultraviolet electronic transitions are shifted into the optical range, allowing them to be observed in quasar spectra with large ground-based telescopes such as Keck and the VLT.

02

Physical conditions in the cold interstellar medium

I am interested in measuring the physical conditions of the ISM in distant galaxies using the rotational excitation of molecules (H2, CO, and HD) and the fine-structure excitation of atoms and ions (C I, C II, and Si II*) observed along quasar sightlines.

I developed software that compares the measured excitation with models of ISM clouds calculated using the Meudon PDR code. This method enables us to infer the gas number density, kinetic temperature, interstellar ultraviolet radiation-field intensity, and cosmic-ray ionization rate (Klimenko & Balashev 2020, MNRAS, 498, 1531; Klimenko et al. 2024, MNRAS, 533, 1367).

  • H2, CO, HD
  • C I, C II, Si II*
  • Meudon PDR code

03

Physical conditions across galaxy environments

I have compared the physical conditions of the ISM in the Milky Way, the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds, and distant galaxies observed along quasar sightlines.

We found that gas in the LMC and SMC is denser and exposed to stronger UV radiation than gas in the Galactic ISM, while distant galaxies show even more extreme physical conditions (Kosenko, Balashev & Klimenko 2024, MNRAS, 528, 5065).

04

The temperature of the cosmic microwave background at z ≈ 1.7–3

In cold, diffuse gas the low rotational levels of CO are populated largely by CMB photons. Measuring CO excitation in distant absorbers therefore provides a direct thermometer for the CMB in the early Universe — a test of the standard-cosmology prediction TCMB(z) = T0(1 + z).

The key is to account for collisional excitation, which I constrain independently from H2 and C I in the same gas (Klimenko et al. 2020, Astronomy Letters, 46, 715).

  • CO excitation
  • Cosmology
  • VLT / ESPRESSO
05

Absorber host galaxies with integral-field spectroscopy

Absorber host galaxies are often missed because they lie outside the narrow slit centred on the background quasar. Integral-field spectroscopy solves this problem by obtaining spectra across a wide field of view, allowing faint galaxies to be identified and studied even at large separations from the quasar sightline. Combined with absorption-line measurements, these data show how gas is distributed around galaxies and how it is affected by star formation, outflows, and inflows.

I collaborate on two projects that use absorption and integral-field spectroscopy to study the distribution of gas around galaxies. The first uses HST/COS spectroscopy of background quasars probing nearby SDSS/MaNGA galaxies (z = 0.02–0.05) with detailed IFU maps (Klimenko et al. 2023, ApJ, 954, 115). The second combines VLT/MUSE and Keck IFU observations (KCWI and OSIRIS) to identify and characterize the host galaxies of strong damped Lyα absorbers along quasar sightlines.

06

Dust grains in distant galaxies

Dust grains absorb and scatter quasar light, producing reddened spectra and broad absorption features that probe their composition and size distribution: the UV 2175 Å feature traces small carbonaceous grains, while the 10 and 18 µm silicate features trace larger silicate grains. Ongoing JWST/MIRI MRS programs in Cycles 1 and 3 use these features to characterize dust in intervening galaxies. Initial Cycle 1 results suggest that galaxies at z = 0.5–1 may contain more silicate-rich dust and have grain-size distributions distinct from those in the local ISM (Klimenko et al. 2026, ApJ, 998, 299; Klimenko et al. 2026, ApJ, 1003, 129).

  • JWST / MIRI MRS
  • Silicates
