Research project 01

Molecular gas in the early Universe

H2, HD, CO, and neutral-carbon absorption in damped Lyα systems at z ≈ 2–3.

Research overview

Molecular absorbers — H2, HD, and CO — reveal cold, dense gas in the interstellar medium of distant galaxies. Compared with local ISM gas, they show distinct abundance patterns, reflecting differences in the physical conditions between the Milky Way and galaxies at high redshift (Klimenko et al. 2016; Klimenko & Balashev 2020; Klimenko et al. 2024).

Molecules are of special interest because they also probe cosmology and fundamental physics. For example:

  • HD/H2 at cosmic noon can serve as a probe of the primordial deuterium abundance (Ivanchik et al. 2015);
  • CO rotational levels are strongly populated by CMB radiation at z ≈ 1–3, which allows us to measure the cosmological evolution of the CMB temperature (Klimenko et al. 2020);
  • variation of the fine-structure constant α and the proton-to-electron mass ratio μ can be constrained from the relative wavelengths of absorption lines compared with those measured in the laboratory (Martins & Pinho 2017).

Searching for new molecular absorbers in SDSS

Molecular absorbers are rare, appearing in fewer than ∼0.5% of quasar spectra. We conducted a systematic search for H2 absorption systems in low-resolution quasar spectra from the SDSS and doubled the number of known high-redshift molecular absorbers at that time (Balashev et al. 2014). We were awarded VLT time to confirm the candidates with VLT/X-shooter (Balashev et al. 2019) and to follow up extremely strong H2 absorption systems with VLT/UVES. This led to the discovery of the most saturated H2 absorber known to date, which represents a large reservoir of CO-dark molecular gas in a galaxy disk at z = 2.79 (Balashev et al. 2017).

Probing physical conditions

Absorption spectroscopy allows precise measurements of the structure of interstellar gas along the line of sight: the velocities of individual clouds, the abundances of different species, and the excitation of their energy levels. Molecules are particularly valuable because they provide precise diagnostics of the physical conditions in the ISM.

The populations of molecular rotational levels (H2, HD, CO) and of the fine-structure levels of neutral carbon (C I) are sensitive to collisional excitation and to indirect excitation through UV pumping. They therefore provide estimates of the gas number density, kinetic temperature, UV radiation field, and cosmic-ray ionization rate (see Physical conditions in the cold interstellar medium).

Fundamental physics and cosmology

Absorption spectroscopy of distant quasars is also a unique tool for probing fundamental physics and cosmology: it constrains possible space-time variation of the fundamental constants over cosmological scales and the evolution of the cosmic microwave background (CMB) temperature with redshift. Variation of the fine-structure constant α and the proton-to-electron mass ratio μ can be constrained from the relative wavelengths of absorption lines compared with those measured in the laboratory (Martins & Pinho 2017). The evolution of the CMB temperature, in turn, is constrained by measuring the excitation of atomic fine-structure or molecular rotational levels whose energy separation is close to the typical energy of CMB photons (Klimenko et al. 2020; see The temperature of the CMB at z ≈ 1.7–3).

Precision spectroscopy with ESPRESSO

Over the past two decades, VLT/UVES and Keck/HIRES have been central to precision measurements using quasar absorption lines, but classical high-resolution spectroscopy is now approaching its limits. A new generation of ultra-stable, high-resolution spectrographs with exquisite wavelength calibration — such as VLT/ESPRESSO and the future ELT/ANDES — will enable the next step in precision measurements. ESPRESSO offers a unique opportunity to reduce statistical uncertainties and instrumental systematics, and to prepare for future observations with the ELT and GMT. My current projects use VLT/ESPRESSO observations of high-redshift molecular absorbers to obtain tighter constraints on carbon isotope ratios and on the CMB temperature at cosmic noon.

Probing the geometry and structure of quasar emission regions

Molecular absorbing clouds are compact, with sizes of ∼1–10 pc, and in rare cases they only partially cover the quasar emission region in the continuum and/or the broad emission lines. This effect produces a non-zero residual flux in the cores of saturated molecular lines, which significantly complicates the analysis. We were the first to identify this effect in high-redshift molecular absorbers (Balashev et al. 2011; Klimenko et al. 2015; Ofengeim et al. 2015), and we use it to estimate the probability of partial covering and the sizes of the quasar emission regions (Klimenko, Petitjean & Ivanchik 2020).
